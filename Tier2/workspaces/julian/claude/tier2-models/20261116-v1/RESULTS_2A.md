# Tier 2A results: investment in new generation

中文版：[RESULTS_2A_ZH.md](RESULTS_2A_ZH.md)

## Problem and model

Beyond the Tier 1 electrolyser and battery, should the client also invest in new generation, in which technology, and how much?

- **Decision variables**: new MW of offshore wind, onshore wind and solar; electrolyser MW; battery MWh; daily electricity sales, hydrogen production, charging and discharging.
- **Objective**: maximise annualised net gain (revenue minus the capital annuity and fixed operating cost).
- **Constraints**: daily energy balance, sales no greater than demand, the hydrogen offtake limit, and the capital budget.
- **Policy A** is a linear programme. Under **policy B** (supply first), whether a day has surplus depends on the new capacity. It is solved by fixed-point iteration on the set of surplus days, and the result is then evaluated exactly with a closed-form daily dispatch.
- With zero new capacity the model reproduces Tier 1.

## Inputs

Costs are taken from Arup's 2024 cost update for DESNZ (2023 prices, central scenario) and converted to 2020 pounds:

| Technology | Total capital cost £/kW | Annual operating cost £k/MW | Net load factor | Life (years) | Levelised cost in this model £/MWh |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Offshore wind | 3,976 | 138.5 | 50.5% | 35 | 72 |
| Onshore wind | 1,588 | 40.1 | 38.1% | 35 | 34 |
| Solar | 659 | 9.3 | 12.2% | 38 | 38 |

The daily output shapes come from the course data. Offshore wind uses the simulated profile of the client's wind farm. Onshore wind and solar use the national by-fuel series, divided by each year's mean to remove capacity growth. All three are then scaled to the load factors in the table.

Each MWh generated by the three technologies actually sells for about £56–62, below the output-weighted price of £80–82. The reason is that electricity generated on surplus days (21% of days) cannot be sold, and windy days are mostly surplus days.

## Main results

**1. The money should go into onshore wind, not offshore.** Offshore wind is zero at every budget. Each additional GW of offshore wind loses about £60m a year, because its cost (£72/MWh) is above the revenue it can realise (£62/MWh).

**2. The optimal portfolio with a £500m budget** (the order of magnitude the client gave in the launch lecture):

| | Policy A | Policy B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Onshore wind | 352 MW | 352 MW |
| Electrolyser | 23.3 MW | 23.3 MW |
| Battery | 0 | 0 |
| Annualised net gain | £44.14m | £34.19m |
| Of which above Tier 1 | £31.40m | £31.73m |
| Simple payback | 6.6 years | 7.6 years |

**3. Diminishing returns are clear.** The annual net return on each additional £1 invested is about 6.9% for the first £100m, about 5.3% between £1bn and £2bn, about 2.8% between £2bn and £4bn, and about 0.5% above £4bn. With no budget limit the model stops at 3.4 GW of onshore wind plus 1.8 GW of solar (£5.6bn, net gain £190m a year); anything more is a net loss. Solar enters only after onshore wind saturates, because it is negatively correlated with existing generation (−0.50) and can sell on the days when wind cannot.

**4. New generation does not change the flexibility assets.** At every budget the electrolyser is 23.3 MW and the battery is zero. The electrolyser's size is set by the hydrogen offtake, not by the amount of electricity.

**5. Generation and hydrogen compete for the budget only when the offtake grows.** When the offtake rises from 10 t/day to 100 t/day, policy A with a £500m budget first builds a 233 MW electrolyser, onshore wind falls to 121 MW, and the annual net gain rises to £137m.

## What would change the conclusion

| Assumption | Choice with a £500m budget (policy A) | Net gain £m/yr |
|---|---|---:|
| Central scenario | 352 MW onshore wind | 44.1 |
| Arup low-cost scenario | 451 MW onshore wind | 52.2 |
| Arup high-cost scenario | Switches to 710 MW solar | 27.9 |
| Electricity price ×0.75 | Unchanged | 28.8 |
| Electricity price ×0.5 | **No generation built** | 18.2 |
| Excluding 2021–2022 | Unchanged | 24.8 |
| Generation capital cost +30% | 271 MW onshore wind | 30.5 |
| Discount rate 8% | Unchanged | 31.6 |
| Hydrogen netback £4/kg | 378 MW onshore wind, **no electrolyser** | 33.3 |

- The conclusion is most sensitive to the electricity price. Onshore wind breaks even when the price falls to about 56% of the actual 2020–2025 mean.
- The high prices of 2021–2022 contribute almost half of the gain. Excluding them reduces the annual net gain from £44.1m to £24.8m, but the choice does not change.

## Limitations

- **Price-taker assumption.** New wind at GW scale would depress the electricity price. The model does not allow for this, so the gains at large budgets are optimistic.
- **The sales limit is national demand.** This follows the Tier 1 system boundary. The GW-scale answer without a budget limit is a product of that boundary, not a recommendation for the client. The budget-constrained results are more credible.
- **The onshore wind and solar shapes come from national series**, not from specific Scottish sites. Projects under construction in the planning database have not been used as a capacity limit.
- **The Arup high-cost scenario** ties high cost to a high load factor, so it is not a purely pessimistic case.
