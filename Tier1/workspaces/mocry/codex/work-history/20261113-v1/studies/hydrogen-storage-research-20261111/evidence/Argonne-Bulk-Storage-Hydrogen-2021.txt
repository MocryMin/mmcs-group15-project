 5   Abstract

 6   The technical aspects and economics of bulk hydrogen storage in underground pipes, lined rock

 7   caverns (LRC) and salt caverns are analyzed. Hydrogen storage in underground pipes is more

 8   economical than in geological caverns for usable amounts <20-t-H2. However, because the pipe

 9    material is a major cost factor, the capital and operating costs for this storage method do not

10    decrease appreciably with an increase in the amount of stored H2. Unlike underground pipes, the

11    installed capital cost of salt caverns decreases appreciably from ~$95/kg-H2 at 100 t-H2 stored to

12   <$19/kg-H2 at 3,000 t-H2 stored. Over the same scale, the annual storage cost decreases from

13   ~$17/kg-H2 to ~$3/kg-H2. Like salt caverns, the installed capital cost of lined rock caverns

14    decreases from ~$160/kg-H2 at 100 t-H2 stored to <$44/kg-H2 at 3,000 t-H2 stored. Storing >750-

15     t usable H2 requires multiple caverns. The cost of salt caverns scales more favorably with size

16   because the salt caverns are larger than lined rock caverns and need to be added at a slower rate as

17    the storage capacity is increased.

18   Keywords: Bulk storage of hydrogen, underground hydrogen pipes, hydrogen storage in salt

19    caverns, hydrogen storage in lined rock caverns

20    1. Introduction

21      Massive deployment of hydrogen and fuel cells in use for power generation as well as

22    transportation will necessarily require viable methods to store hydrogen at different time and

23    length scales. Daily and seasonal storage of hydrogen can facilitate large-scale penetration of

24   renewable energy by buffering the fluctuating imbalance between power/fuel production and

                                               2
PAGE 3
 1   demand. In the industrial sector, bulk storage may be required to balance daily demands as well as

 2    to prevent interruption in supply when the plant undergoes maintenance.

 3     At a small scale, hydrogen can be stored compactly as compressed gas at 350-950 bar in Type

 4    2, 3 or 4 tanks. Storing gas at such high pressures requires the tanks to be reinforced with carbon

 5    fibers or pre-stressed concrete, which results in high capital and operating costs [1–4]. Typical

 6    applications of this method are hydrogen storage on board vehicles (5-10 kg at 350-700 bar in

 7   Type 3 or 4 tanks), buffer hydrogen storage at refueling stations (100 kg in Type 2 or 4 tanks), and

 8   tube trailers for hydrogen delivery to refueling stations (1,000 kg capacity, 540 bar, Type 4 tanks)

 9    [5–8].

10     At a medium scale, gaseous hydrogen could potentially be stored in spherical vessels or

11   underground pipe facilities [3,9,10]. Spherical vessels have been utilized in Europe to store natural

12   gas (NG) at a low pressure, ~20 bar, and thus require large spaces. Underground pipe facilities

13   have been applied for seasonal storage of NG in Germany and Switzerland. A new pipe facility in

14   Bocholt-Mussum (Germany) has the capacity to store 350,000 m3 of natural gas at pressures up to

15   90 bar. The advantages of underground pipe storage facilities are the protection from adverse

16   weather conditions and no requirement to have shaft structures, so that the land above ground can

17   be used for other purposes such as farming.

18     At larger scales, hydrogen is stored cryogenically as a liquid at 20 K (LH2) in spherical tanks

19    [3,11–14]. The  largest LH2 storage  facility  at NASA’s (National Aeronautics and Space

20    Administration, U.S.A.) Kennedy Space Center has two 3,200-m3 spheres, 21.3 m in diameter,

21   each capable of storing 230 tonnes of LH2 [13]. There are several issues with LH2, however.

22    Liquefaction of hydrogen is a costly and energy-demanding process [15–20]. State of the art

23    liquefaction plants, based on a hydrogen compression/expansion sequence with a pre-cooling step,



                                               3
PAGE 4
 1   consume 10-12 kWh/kg-H2, which corresponds to one third of the lower heating value (LHV) of

 2   hydrogen and incurs ~$1.40/kg-H2 levelized capital cost. That cost increases to ~$2.75/kg-H2, if

 3    the cost of electricity is included [21]. Even though the cryogenic tanks are vacuum insulated, on

 4   a stored volume basis, the typical daily boil-off rate is 0.03% for the large spherical tank at the

 5  NASA complex, increasing to 1% for smaller cylindrical tanks [22,23]. Mitigation strategies exist

 6    to reduce boil-off by placing a liquid nitrogen shield between the insulation or using a closed-loop

 7   helium refrigeration system with an internal heat exchanger in the tank [24].

 8      Underground geological storage is expected to be the most economic method for storage at

 9    substantial amounts of hydrogen [4,25–29]. It has been used widely, primarily in the natural gas

10   and  oil  industry,  for meeting seasonal and peak demands. The U.S. Energy Information

11   Administration (EIA) estimates that there are 379 active underground fields for NG storage in the

12    U.S., with ~3.8 trillion cubic feet of working gas capacity [30]. Eighty-eight percent of the

13    operating storage is provided by depleted natural gas fields, 9% by aquifer reservoirs and 3% by

14    salt caverns (although they account for 15% of the delivered capacity).

15      Conceptual studies have evaluated geological storage of gaseous hydrogen in depleted oil and

16   gas fields, aquifers and salt caverns [31–37]. To date, hydrogen storage has been demonstrated in

17    salt caverns [9], but not in depleted fields or aquifers, which require a caprock to seal hydrogen.

18   The high diffusivity of hydrogen can result in losses in porous underground formations. In case of

19    aquifers, hydrogen could potentially be lost by dissolving in the large contact area with the

20   surrounding brine. This may be relevant, especially under circumstances of elevated temperature

21   and excessive pressures [34,37]. Other issues with porous underground storage are the potential

22    interactions between hydrogen and hydrocarbons dissolved in reservoir rocks through microbial

23   metabolism [37–39]. In the presence of CO, CO2 or sulfates, hydrogen can convert to CH4 and



                                               4
PAGE 5
 1   H2S. Other potential issues are hydrogen reacting chemically (or electrochemically) with mineral

 2    deposits and initiating adverse changes in porosity and permeability [34]. Also, if hydrogen

 3   becomes contaminated, a separation step is required to provide fuel cell-quality hydrogen to the

 4   consumer.

 5       Salt caverns are manmade artificial cavities with some unique properties of gas tightness and

 6    inertness. Depending on the depth of the cavity, storage pressures up to 25 MPa are feasible as are

 7    large capacities exceeding 1 million m3. Pure hydrogen has been successfully stored without losses

 8    in salt caverns at three locations [9]: three caverns in Teesside, U.K. since 1972 (200,000 m3), one

 9    in Clemens, TX since 1983 (580,000 m3), and a more recent one in Moss, Texas since 2007

10   (566,000 m3). Large salt formations are geographically limited, however. In the U.S., large salt

11   bodies are primarily located in the Gulf Coast [35].

12     Where salt formations are not available, rock caverns can provide more possibilities close to

13   hydrogen production  facilities. Rock caverns have been constructed  for storage of liquid

14   hydrocarbons and natural gas and conceptually investigated for compressed air [40,41]. The

15   caverns are constructed through typical mining operations. The concept of unlined rock caverns

16    requires a competent rock with low permeability, free of fractures and fissures. Sufficient

17   groundwater is needed to seal the rock or a water curtain must be applied in the surroundings for

18   proper tightness [41]. However, the over-pressurized water system required around the caverns

19    significantly increases the inflow of water to the caverns and may result in losses caused by

20    dissolved gases dissolved in this water. In the absence of a low permeability rock mass, a tight

21    storage medium can be constructed by cladding the cavern with a liner, which can be a polymer

22   membrane or thin steel sheets [40,42–44]. The liner seals the gas whereas the surrounding rock

23   absorbs the load. The host rock must be of sufficient quality as it determines the maximum storage



                                               5
PAGE 6
 1    pressure and consequently the storage volume and cost of mining. Lined rock caverns have been

 2    successfully demonstrated in a pilot project in Scandinavia in early 2000 to store natural gas [45];

 3   however, no commercial facility has been built to date.

 4      This work analyzes the technical aspects and cost effectiveness of different methods for bulk

 5    storage of gaseous hydrogen. Figure 1 identifies the storage methods of interest and their relevance

 6    to different applications. Confining the scope of the work, we only consider the large-scale gaseous

 7    storage options suitable for outages of hydrogen production plants (10-30 days of daily capacity)

 8   and seasonal variations in hydrogen demand. We consider two geological storage options, salt

 9   caverns and lined rock caverns, and one geographically agnostic underground pipe storage method.

10   Bottoms-up cost analyses are performed for each case and the results are discussed below and in

11    the supplemental document.

12    2. Methodology

13    2.1 Bulk H2 Storage in Underground Pipes

14   Figure 2 presents a schematic of the core cost characteristics constructing the pipe facility.

15      The main cost components are a) pipe production at the mill, b) surface coating of the pipes,

16    c) transmission of pipes by rail and truck to the staging area, d) site preparation (excavation and

17    backfilling), and e) pipe installation, welding, weld inspection, and final pressure testing for

18    integrity.

19       Starting with the production of the pipes, electric resistance welded (ERW) pipes were assumed

20    in this analysis [46]. We also considered pipes with different diameters and wall thicknesses (18-

21   36" O.D., schedule 10-100) as to determine the minimum cost. Steel plates are trimmed to proper

22   dimensions, rolled into a pipe section and resistance welded. The pipe is cut in segments of 12 m,

23   heated in a furnace and allowed to gradually cool as to temper the steel. Pipes are inspected and

24    hydrostatically tested and packaged before delivery from the plant. Supplemental A details the cost

                                               6

 1    of the pipes for different diameters and schedules investigated in this study. The cost of the pipes,

 2   normalized in terms of mass, ranges between $1,090/MT to $1,371/MT. These estimates compare

 3    well with the costs of similar pipes (>24") used in the natural gas and oil industry (1,080-1644

 4   $/MT) [47]. The variance is attributed to the cost of steel which accounts for ~64% of the cost of

 5    the pipe.

 6     The pipes are packaged and transported from the production mill to the nearest coating plant

 7    for corrosion resistance. A three-layer polyethylene coating is sufficient for corrosion prevention,

 8   without the need to install more expensive cathodic protection systems. The coated pipes are

 9    transported by rail to and from the coating plant and locally by trucks to the storage site. For our

10   base case scenario, a 24" pipe schedule 60 can store hydrogen at a maximum pressure of 100 bar,

11   and ~24,000 pipe segments are required for storing 500 tonnes of hydrogen. This number of pipes

12   may require purchase orders from several mills and a broker to facilitate the procurement,

13    inspection and other logistics. The cost contribution of coating and logistics [47] are summarized

14    in Table 1.

 1      Lined rock caverns have as to date not been used for storage in industrial scale, however, pilot

 2    projects have confirmed its viability for NG storage with sufficient construction details [45,51,52].

 3   Figure 3a shows the principle cost features of this type of storage system and consists of four parts:

 4   excavation of the underground  facility including access tunnels and the cavern enclosure,

 5    installation of the liner and concrete surrounding the liner and the cavern forming the storage

 6    facility and the aboveground facilities including compressor, pipes and valves.

 7     The excavation of the rock cavern consists of one or multiple cavern systems with an

 8   arrangement of shafts and tunnels joining them with the ground surface. The excavation is

 9   performed by conventional drill-and-blast operations [52,53]. The cross-sections of the shafts and

10    tunnels are 25 m2. The total length of the tunnels is 760 m (a 400 m access tunnel, two 80 m tunnels

11   connecting to the lower and upper part of the dome and a 200 m shaft). The tunneling operations

12    also include widenings periodically as to facilitate the turning of mining vehicles. This adds

13    another 20% to the total length of the tunnels. Rock support is used in sections of the tunnels (30%)

14   with grouted rebar. Grouting is also performed to minimize groundwater leaking and allows for

15   more efficient pumping equipment. The caverns are excavated as hemispherical cylinders and

16    reinforced with rock bolting and concrete. The dimensions of a cavern capable of storing 500 tonne

17    of H2 at a pressure of 150 bar are 62 m in diameter and 71 m in height. The excavated rock is

18   assumed to have no monetary value, and the rocks are trucked 10 miles to remote deposit sites.

19   Cost contributions of mining the tunnels and cavern with additional work to fortify the walls are

20    detailed in Supplemental B.

21      Figure 3b shows some important technical details of the construction and structural properties

22    for LRC storage. The lining represents a key component to ensure a proper gas storage and

23    structural integrity. The steel liner contains the gas whereas the sealing layers transmits the



                                               9
PAGE 10
 1    pressure load to the surrounding rock [42,54,55]. The lining consists of 15-mm thick mild steel

 2    sheets which are welded together to form the inner vessel of the dome. The concrete layer (2-m

 3    thick), reinforced with a rebar lattice, distributes the pressure load to the surrounding rock. A

 4    drainage arrangement of perforated pipes is also mounted around the cavern before the concrete is

 5   poured as to remove water accumulating in the lining section. The maximum pressure of the gas

 6    that can be stored depends on the cavern depth and the quality of the rock mass [42]. A depth of

 7   150 m can typically a withstand pressure of 300 bar and higher preventing the rock mass from

 8    uplifting [52]. The surrounding rock mass must be strong enough as to not be deformed by the

 9    pressure load and cause fracture of the lining. This is determined by the rock mass rating (RMR)

10   and thus a geological survey is imperative to locate the best site for the cavern [56,57]. The last

11   panel in Figure 3b correlates the RMR method with the maximum cavern storage pressure. For our

12   base case scenario, we consider that the cavern is located 150 m below the surface and assume an

13  RMR value to the rock of 50, which limits the maximum storage pressure to 150 bar. The costs

14    associated in commissioning the cavern to a fully functional storage system are detailed in

15   Supplemental B.

16       Finally, the above-bound facility consists of a compressor and balance of plant components

17    (e.g. piping and valves) similar to that for pipe storage systems. We assume that the hydrogen

18   production facility is located one mile from the storage site and a pipeline is constructed with right-

19   of-way associated costs [58]. Table 3 presents the capital, operational and maintenance costs for

20    the aboveground facility.

21    2.3 Bulk H2 Storage in Underground Salt Caverns





                                              10
PAGE 11
 1       Salt caverns are especially suitable for low-cost hydrogen storage in extensive quantities, but

 2    are geographically limited to a few locations; see Figure 4. Favorable locations in the U.S. are

 3   found in the Gulf Coast and the northeast.

 4      Figure 5 shows the main cost contributions considered in the development of the salt cavern

 5    storage. Costs are associated with leaching out the cavern with fresh water at sufficient depth and

 6    size, brine transportation and disposal costs and the surface-bound facility.

 7      Before the salt cavern is constructed, a geological survey is necessary to ensure that the mining

 8    will not threaten the integrity of the containing salt. Three-dimensional mapping and seismic

 9   surveying are common geophysical methods. An exploratory drilling is finally conducted to

10    explore porosity and permeability, stress measurements and the presence of corrosive substances

11   and the adherence of the salt dome [9,59].

12      Meeting all requirements, the underground cavern can be created using a process known as

13    solution mining or leaching. Solution mining technology converts a simple borehole into a storage

14   cavern through the injection and extraction of water. First, a borehole from the surface to the final

15    required depth is drilled through the salt formation. This process is very similar to the established

16    oil and gas drilling practice [9,60,61]. After drilling, an annular pipe is fitted through the borehole

17   and lowered to the middle of the cavity. To form the salt cavern, fresh water is pumped through

18    the inner tubing to the bottom of the borehole. The water dissolves the salt and saturated brine is

19   removed through the other pipe, leaving an area of free space. Water supply for the leaching

20    process is assumed to be available from a nearby well (2 miles), and pipelines are required to

21   supply the water during the leaching operation. Approximately 7-8 m3 of fresh water is needed to

22    dissolve 1 m3 of salt [62]. Brine is stored on site for up to 2 days of operation and is disposed by

23    trucks into deep Class II wells [63,64]. It is possible to recover salt from the brine as a chemical



                                              11
PAGE 12
 1    feedstock (e.g., for the chlor-alkali process); however, the facilities need to be in close proximity

 2    to the storage site for this to be economically viable. In absence of Class II wells, a more costly

 3    disposal would be trucking the brine to the nearest wastewater treatment plant [65].

 4     Upon completion of the cavern structure, preparation is required to convert from brine to gas

 5    storage. Before any gas is introduced, an initial mechanical integrity test (MIT) is performed to

 6   confirm gas tightness of the uppermost salt and the well completion. The last step in the

 7    construction of the cavern is the debrining process, which essentially uses gas pressure to displace

 8    the brine contained in the cavern at the completion of solution mining. Caverns constructed in salt

 9   domes are typically 60 to 240 m in length, and there is a great deal of flexibility in positioning a

10   cavern depth down to about 1,800 m. The storage pressure increases by 0.156 bar per meter of

11   depth ( [60]. For our base scenario, the cavern was placed 765 meters belowground, requiring a

12   gas pressure of 120 bar to prevent creep of the cavern walls. The cost to construct a salt cavern is

13    detailed in Supplemental C.

14     The surface-bound facility is identical to the LRC and pipe storage facilities, as discussed

15    previously. The exception is that a water-wash and gas-drying step is needed for hydrogen purity

16    control. The cavern contains a heel of brine in the sump (bottom layer of debris), which cannot be

17   removed during the debrining process, therefore gas withdrawn from the cavern will be saturated

18   with salt during an initial period of operation. The water-wash column removes entrained salt, and

19    the dryer (sorbents) removes water in order to meet H2 quality specifications. This adds an

20    operational cost of $44,783/year in desiccant drying costs.

 5    3.2 Bulk H2 Storage in Underground Lined Rock Caverns

 6    We conducted a study to estimate the cost of storing 500 t-H2 in an underground LRC facility

 7   varying the maximum storage pressure while holding the minimum pressure as constant at 20 bar.

 8   The results of the study are summarized in Figure 7 and Table 6. Figure 7 shows that the capital

 9    cost decreases at higher storage pressures; however, the rate of decrease slows at pressures above

10   150-200 bar.

11     At 150-bar storage pressure, the underground facility accounts for 66% of the total capital cost

12    including 34% for cavern excavation, 16% for tunnels, 8% for liner and 8% for concrete. The

13   aboveground facility represents 14% of the total cost including 11% for compressor, 3% for piping

14   and instrumentation and <1% for cushion gas. The miscellaneous items represent 21% of the total

15    cost including 10% for engineering, 6% for contingency, 3% for survey and 2% for permitting.

16   Thus, cavern excavation, tunnels, compressor and engineering are the biggest cost items.

17     Lowering the storage pressure from 150 bar to 75 bar results in 61% higher capital cost, 82%

18    of which is due to the higher cost of the underground facility which has more than double the

19    storage volume. The aboveground facility cost is ~3% lower primarily because of a smaller

20   compressor bust; this cost saving is offset by a 14% increase in the miscellaneous costs for

21    engineering services and contingency.

22     The capital cost is smallest at 300-bar storage pressure. Compared to the 150-bar storage

23   system, the required storage volume is about 50% smaller resulting in a 21% cost saving for the

 8    3. Conclusions

 9      Massive deployment of hydrogen and fuel cell technologies will require viable methods to store

10   hydrogen at different time and length scales. We have analyzed the technical aspects and

11   economics of bulk hydrogen storage in underground pipes, lined rock caverns and salt caverns in

12    different quantities, 1-3,000 tonnes, equivalent to <1 to 60 days of storage for a 50 tonnes per day

13   production facility. The main conclusions are summarized below.

14   ▪ Hydrogen storage in underground Type-1, API 5L X52 pipes is more economical than in

15       geological caverns for usable amounts <20-t-H2. At ~$560/kg-H2 stored, the estimated capital

16       cost is high as is the annual cost at ~$84/kg-H2 stored. Because the pipe material is a major cost

17       factor, the capital and operating costs for this storage method do not decrease appreciably with

18      an increase in the amount of stored H2.

19   ▪  Unlike underground pipes, the installed capital cost of salt caverns decreases appreciably from

20      ~$95/kg-H2 at 100 t-H2 stored to <$19/kg-H2 at 3,000 t-H2 stored. Over the same scale, the

21      annual storage cost decreases from ~$17/kg-H2 at 100 t-H2 stored to ~$3/kg-H2 at 3,000 t-H2

22       stored.





                                              19
PAGE 20
 1   ▪  Like salt caverns, the installed capital cost of lined rock caverns decreases from ~$160/kg-H2

 2        at 100 t-H2 stored to <$44/kg-H2 at 3,000 t-H2 stored. Over the same scale, the annual storage

 3       cost decreases from ~$26/kg-H2 at 100 t-H2 stored to ~$7/kg-H2 at 3,000 t-H2 stored.

 4   ▪  Storing >750-t usable H2 requires multiple caverns. Hydrogen storage in salt caverns is more

 5      economical than in lined rock caverns. Also, the cost of salt caverns scales more favorably with

 6       size because the salt caverns are larger than lined rock caverns and need to be added at a slower

 7       rate as the storage capacity is increased.
