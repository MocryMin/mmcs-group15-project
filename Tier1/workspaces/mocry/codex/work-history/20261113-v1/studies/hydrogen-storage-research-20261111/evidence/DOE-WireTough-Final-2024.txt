                                  Abstract

      Long-duration energy storage (LDES) in the form of gaseous hydrogen is vital for
fossil fuel power plants to reduce frequent shutdowns and rapid start-ups. It's also crucial
in renewable energy power plants for grid stabilization. WireTough Cylinders (WTC) has
developed a Type II-S pressure vessel that uses steel liners that are hoop-wrapped with
multiple layers of high-strength, cost-effective, and readily available steel wires. This
design enables the vessel to withstand significantly higher pressures.  It addresses
hydrogen embrittlement issues and cyclic loading from repeated pressurization and
depressurization during service. The WireTough design is cost-efficient, robust, and
fatigue-resistant (with a 30-year design life) and is ASME certified for ground storage of
hydrogen at a maximum operating pressure (MOP) of 350 bar and a design pressure of
500 bar. The cylinder has a 645 mm outside diameter and a water capacity of 1,715 liters,
capable of storing 40.7 KG of hydrogen at MOP. Two prototypes were successfully
produced to showcase the manufacturing technologies, with one being certified under
ASME Section VIII Division 3 rules. Preliminary design work for a similar Type II-S vessel
with a 500 bar MOP was also completed. The 350 bar and 500 bar Type II-S vessels
were shown respectively to be 47.5% to 60% more cost-effective and 55% and 62.5%
lighter based on per KG of hydrogen stored, when compared with  their Type 1
counterparts.

                            Executive Summary
        Fossil-fuel electricity generating plants rely on capital-intensive equipment that
must frequently be shut down and rapidly ramped up to accommodate varying energy
demands throughout the day. The resulting thermal cycling causes damage to plant
components, leading to costly, unplanned outages and inefficient energy conversion. This
problem underscores the importance of integrating environmentally friendly on-site long
duration energy storage (LDES) facility within the plant. LDES facilities are also needed
for balancing the power grid in renewable energy power plants and at fueling stations
serving various transportation needs. Storing gaseous hydrogen at high pressures (35-
50 MPa) in specially designed pressure vessels is an attractive LDES solution.

      WireTough  Cylinders  (WTC)  has  developed  an  innovative  design  and
manufacturing  technologies   for  cost-effective,  durable,  fatigue  and  hydrogen-
embrittlement  resistant, ASME-Section VIII-Division 3  certified cylinders  for storing
hydrogen at high pressures. These cylinders feature a seamless steel liner encased in a
steel wire jacket composed of multiple layers of ultra-high-strength steel wires with tensile
strength exceeding 2.0 GPa. These wires are meticulously wound around the liner's axial
length and secured with epoxy. After wire wrapping, the cylinders undergo autofrettage,
a process that imparts high compressive stresses throughout the liner wall at ambient
pressure. This significantly enhances the fatigue life of the cylinders and strengthens the
bond at the interface of the steel wire jacket and the liner.

    The product lineup comprises three models of all-steel Type II-S cylinders, each
designed for specific pressure ratings. These models store 40.7 KG of hydrogen at 350
bars, 42 KG at 500 bars, and 29.5 KG at 700 bars in a single vessel. The 350-bar cylinder
has an outer diameter of 645 mm, measures 7600 mm in length, and has a capacity of
1715 liters. This design is fully certified. The 500-bar cylinder features an outer diameter
of 543 mm, a length of 9000 mm, and a capacity of 1400 liters, while the 700-bar cylinder
has an outer diameter of 441 mm, a length of 9000 mm, and a capacity of 765 liters. The
latter two designs are ready for certification but have not yet gone through the certification
steps.

     In-depth finite element analyses were conducted on both the steel liner and the
finished pressure vessels, ensuring compliance with the relevant sections of the ASME
code. These analyses encompassed stresses originating from the wire-winding process,
residual stresses from autofrettage, and subsequent service loading. Fracture mechanics
analyses, in line with ASME Section VIII Division 3 Codes, were employed to estimate
the cyclic life of the cylinder. Assessments involved postulating surface flaws measuring
1 mm in depth and 5 mm in length at various locations within the liner to assess the
vessel's integrity.

     The 1715 L capacity liners for producing cylinders with a 350-bar operating
pressure rating were obtained from Tenaris, USA. These liners fully adhered to the design
specifications  to produce  full-size  prototypes, which  effectively demonstrated  the
manufacturing technology. Two cylinders underwent all steps of wire wrapping. One of
                                         9
PAGE 11
these wrapped cylinders was also subjected to autofrettage and hydrotesting and was
certified by an ASME authorized inspector, affirming  its compliance with  all design
requirements.

      The process also included the formulation of a User Design Specification (UDS)
to clearly define the cylinder's capabilities for service. Subsequently, a Manufacturer's
Design Report (MDR), developed by Structural Integrity Associates, was produced to
fulfill the UDS requirements. This report evaluated the WTC design against all ASME
Section VIII-Div 3 codes, confirming full compliance.

     The MDR revealed that the knuckle region on the inner surface at the intersection
between the nozzle and the dome is the critical, life-limiting region of the liner. In this
region, liner wall thicknesses were found to be more than 60% greater than initially
assumed in stress estimations. Factoring in these realistic thicknesses, the vessel's
design life was estimated at 6,000 cycles, equivalent to 30 years at a rate of 200 cycles
per year. Design lives in other areas were higher. Also, the critical region is not covered
by the wire jacket and therefore accessible for inspection while in service to potentially
extend the cylinder life beyond the 30 years of design life.

    The manufacturing costs for the 1715 L, 350-bar cylinder were estimated. The costs
were adversely affected by the doubling of steel prices and increased shipping costs
during the COVID-19 pandemic. In scenarios where fewer than 20 cylinders are produced
annually, manufacturing costs were estimated at $1478 per kilogram of stored hydrogen.
However, this cost significantly reduces to $827 per kilogram of hydrogen stored—a 44%
reduction—when  annual  production  volume  (APV)  increases  to  200. A  single
manufacturing line operating in two shifts can achieve an annual production of 200
cylinders, so this cost is achievable. These costs surpass the goals set by the Department
of Energy (DOE) for bulk hydrogen storage.

    The technology readiness level (TRL) for this innovation was raised from TRL-4 to
TRL-8 during the program. The 350-bar, 1715 L hydrogen storage cylinder is now eligible
for commercial deployment, and the 500-bar and 700-bar cylinders can be brought to the
commercial market within six-months to one year.

                                    1. Introduction

       Capital intensive equipment in fossil-fuel electricity generating plants is required
to shut-down frequently and ramp-up rapidly to manage variable energy demands within
a single day. Damage to plant equipment/components due to such thermal cycling
causes unplanned outages that can be expensive and results in inefficient energy
conversion. Integrating an onsite environment-friendly long term energy storage (LDES)
facility addresses this challenge. Utilizing excess electricity during low demand periods
in fossil-fuel plants or during peak generation periods in renewable energy plants to
generate hydrogen and storing  it at high pressures of 350-500 bar  is attractive.
Advances in pressure vessel technology have made this energy storage approach
technically viable and cost-effective on a large scale [1]. Other approaches include
liquification of hydrogen, but that requires large amounts of energy to initially cool the
gas and then to maintain the low temperatures during storage. Metal hydrides require
considerable heat to discharge the stored hydrogen and the process is slow. Surface
hydrogen storage systems such as C-nanotubes are currently not sufficiently mature
[2]. The compressed H2 approach to LDES also competes favorably with non-hydrogen
approaches such as batteries that degrade over time, retain energy only for short
duration, and create chemical waste at the end of life. The pumped water approach
requires large amounts of land, and thermal energy storage systems are currently not
efficient.

      WireTough Cylinders (WTC) has developed the design and manufacturing
technologies for low-cost, durable, and fatigue damage-resistant ASME BPVC Section
VIII-Division 3 certified cylinders to store hydrogen at high pressures. WireTough’s
solution consists of a patented design, and proprietary manufacturing processes
utilizing a seamless steel liner and a steel wire jacket made from multiple layers of ultra-
high strength (> 2.0 GPa), automotive tire chord quality steel wires that are wrapped
around the axial length of the liner and held together with an epoxy. A schematic drawing
of WireTough’s Type II-S design is shown in Fig. 1.1. The wire jacket carries a significant
portion of the pressure load. After wire wrapping, the cylinders are subjected to an
autofrettage process which locks high compressive stresses throughout the thickness
of the liner wall. The process significantly improves the fatigue life of the cylinders and
simultaneously strengthens the interface bond between the steel wire jacket and the
liner that prevents moisture from seeping in during service. Efficient cylinders can be
designed to operate at maximum operating pressures in the range of 350 to 900 bar.
  One KG of hydrogen contains the heat equivalent of 39 kWh of electricity and
assuming an efficiency of 60% for fuel cells, it will yield back an estimated 23.4 kWh of
electricity. A 1715-liter cylinder that has an OD of 645 mm and a length of 7600 mm
and can safely withstand a hydrogen pressure that cycles between 350 and 35 bar can
store about 40.7 KG of hydrogen that can deliver approximately 950 KWh of electricity.
A modular design will permit building energy banks of different capacities. For example,
a six-cylinder stack (Fig. 1.2) can build an energy bank that can store 5.4 MWh of power
in a module with a footprint of less than 200 Sq. ft.

2.1 Objective 1: Selection of Cylinder Sizes and Performance Specifications

   The cylinder specifications were selected after consideration of several factors
including manufacturability, maintainability during service, cost, and durability. These are
as follows:

•  Maximum  operating  pressures (MOP)  of  350,  500, and 700  bar based on
   considerations of volumetric energy density of hydrogen and maximizing cylinder
   capacity at those pressures.

•  Choice of liners made from commonly available pipe sizes with outside diameters of
   610, 508, and 406 mm.

•  Stress transfer ratio from the liner wall to the wire jacket to optimize autofrettage
   pressures necessary for optimum fatigue durability during service and reducing risk of
    failure in the dome region of the vessel during autofrettage.

•  The maximum length of the pressure vessel to be 10,000 mm or less based on limits
    of transportation, maximum finished weight of the vessel of less than 4,500 KG due to
   winding machine limitations and the capacity of the material handling systems.

•  Amount of hydrogen stored at the maximum operating pressure (MOP) of 40 – 60 KG.

•  Number of wire layers in the steel wire wrap (40 to 48 layers).

•  Use of only ASME Code approved materials for pressure vessels for storing hydrogen,

•  Even though the vessel weight expressed in Kilogram (KG) per KG of H2 stored is not
   a primary consideration for ground storage, the cost, which is a primary consideration,
    is directly linked to the vessel weight.

   These considerations narrowed the choices of liners listed in Table 2.1 and wrapped
cylinder dimensions listed in Table 2.2. Clearly, the ideal pressures for bulk storage are
between 350 to 500 bar from the weight per KG of hydrogen stored and the efficiencies
obtained in transferring stresses on to the high strength wire jacket. Further, a 610 mm
OD liner suitable for producing a wrapped vessel capable of storing 40.7 KG of H2 at 350
bar pressure is the optimum among the three. Thus, the design analysis in the support of
Objective 2 described in the next section will focus on that vessel size.

2.2 Objective 2: Design Analysis

       Finite element analyses were conducted (a) of the steel liner by itself (b) of the
wire wrapped pressure vessel to determine the magnitude of residual stresses created
during wire wrapping and during autofrettage, and (c) of the finished vessel subject to
service loading. Fracture mechanics-based methods that are part of the ASME Section
VIII Division 3 Codes were used to estimate the cyclic life of the cylinder. The results from
these analyses are described in the following sections.

     The  finite element analyses were conducted by Siemens using Siemens’
Simcenter 3D. The simulation workflow was automated using NX Open API for each
design, including  (i) design update,  (ii) setting the appropriate  initial and boundary
conditions, (iii) solve the Simcenter Nastran simulations, and (iv) results extraction and
analysis after each simulation. The design space exploration was performed with
Siemens Simcenter HEEDS that accelerated the design space exploration by enabling
automation of the simulation analysis workflows and enabling distributed execution using
available computational resources.

PAGE 17
  2.2.1  Liner Analyses

     The liner chosen is shown in Fig. 2.1 and was virtually pressurized and the stress
and strain at various points were estimated. The purpose was to estimate the burst
pressure of the proposed liner. The liner material was SA372 grade E or J steel and the
yield strength and the ultimate strength chosen for the analyses were the minimum values
specified for this grade of material. These values were 482.5 MPa and 827 MPa,
respectively. The OD for determining the burst pressure was 617 which is the nominal
OD +1% allowable variation within the liner specifications. The thickness was 22.9 mm
which is the minimum specified thickness. Figure 2.2 shows that an excellent comparison
was obtained between the predicted stress-strain behavior at a highly stressed point in
the liner with the input stress-strain for the liner material. This result validates the accuracy
of the finite element results.

   The predicted burst pressure for the liner by itself was 72 MPa from the above
analysis. Structural Integrity Associates have also conducted an elastic-plastic analysis
of the same liner and have estimated the burst pressure as  64 MPa using a more
conservative failure  criterion based on onset of plastic instability. A third estimate of the
burst pressure, based on the well-known Faupel formula, equation (2.1) yielded the most

  2.2.3  Estimation of the Autofrettage Pressure
   The process of autofrettage is used in the pressure vessel industry to enhance the
fatigue life of vessels for storing hydrogen at high pressures. As the storage pressures
fluctuate during service, the amplitudes of circumferential fatigue stresses also change,
affecting the fatigue life of the vessel. Autofrettage locks high compressive stresses in the
entire liner wall of the vessel that decreases the magnitude of the maximum and the
minimum stresses during the service cycle. This significantly reduces the environment
assisted fatigue crack growth rates that depend on the maximum stress and the stress
ratio, enhancing the cyclic life of the vessel.

  A model to correctly estimate the autofrettage pressure necessary to introduce a
repeatable and consistent residual stress pattern in the liner wall from its ID to OD in Type
II-S vessels was developed. The results from cases listed in Table 2.2 were used to derive
an equation for estimating the autofrettage pressure as a function of the liner wall
thickness, liner material yield strength, the OD, and the wrap thickness.

   Figure 2.4 shows a plot of Von Mises’ equivalent strain on the outer diameter, OD, of
the liner as a function of the applied internal pressure in the wire-wrapped cylinder. The
autofrettage pressure is assumed to have reached when the equivalent strain on the OD
of the liner is equal to the elastic strain for the stress level applied plus a plastic strain of
0.3%, of which 0.2% is associated with reaching plastic yielding plus 0.1% beyond that.
This ensures that the entire liner wall deforms plastically during autofrettage, so when the
pressure is removed, high compressive stresses are locked through the entire cross-
section of the liner. The chosen autofrettage pressure is marked in Fig. 2.4.

strain of 0.3%.

  2.2.4  Estimation of Residual Stresses Following Autofrettage

     The objective of the autofrettage process is to develop a pattern of predictable

during service loading cycle. Since negative stresses do not contribute toward the

linearly between the ID and OD, that is expected for thin-wall liners. Since the autofrettage
pressure is linked to a fixed amount of plastic deformation on the OD, the residual stress
pattern for different thicknesses lies along the same trend. Thus, the magnitude of
residual stresses in the interior of the liner wall do not vary with liner thickness but are

2.4  Summary of Design Analysis and Fracture Mechanics Analyses Results

   Stresses were estimated in the critical region of the vessel subject to service loading
consisting of pressurization and depressurization cycles. The circumferential stresses
near the junction between the liner and the wrap were the highest and were used to
estimate the environment assisted fatigue crack growth life of the vessels. The analyses
lead to the following conclusions.

   •  Autofrettage, used during fabrication of Type II-S pressure vessels to enhance the
       fatigue life of vessels, locks high compressive residual stresses in the vessel’s liner
       wall and reduces the maximum and the mean stress during service loading.
   •  To achieve a consistent residual stress profile across the wall, autofrettage
      pressure must be selected such that there is plastic deformation on the outside
      surface of the liner. The pressure depends on liner wall thickness and the wrap
       thickness, the OD of the liner, and the liner yield strength.
   •  From the finite element analyses, a model was developed to represent the effects
       of these variables on the autofrettage pressure in the form of a simple, closed form
       equation.
   •  The resulting residual stress profiles from autofrettage obtained from finite element
      analyses were used to develop equations for estimating the residual stress
       distribution as a function radial distance along the wall thickness, liner OD, and the
       yield strength of the material.
   •  To achieve a lifetime of 20 years in pressure vessels that will experience an
      average of 200 cycles of pressurization/depressurization cycles per year,  it is
      necessary to limit the maximum operating pressure to 41 MPa (410 bar) for vessels
       with an OD of 617 mm and liner wall thickness of 25.2 mm and to 37.5 (375 bar)
        for vessels with liner wall thickness of 22.9 mm.
                                         41
PAGE 43
   •     It will be shown later in the report that a flaw on the ID in the knuckle region
      between the dome and the nozzle gives the shortest life and limits the MOP to 350
       bar. This additional analysis was performed as part of design certification and
       preparation of the Manufacturer’s Design Report (MDR) described in Section 2.6
       of this report.
   •  To achieve an MOP of 50 MPa, it is necessary to decrease the liner OD to 508
    mm and raise the minimum wall thickness of the liner to 25.2 mm.
   •  To achieve an MOP of 700 bar, the liner OD of the liner should be decreased to
     406 mm and a minimum wall thickness be increased to 31.75 mm.

           support of the manufacturing operation.

        •  Administration and accounting,

        •  Marketing related travel,

        •  Legal and insurance expenses,

        •   Facility rental for required manufacturing space for one line,

        •  Costs of repair and equipment replacement,

        •  Office supplies and communications.

 The fixed annual costs detailed above are distributed equally among cylinders as part
of annual production volume (APV). For estimating cylinder costs, the APV was varied
between 20 to the full capacity of 200 cylinders. Note that the fixed cost does not include
initial equipment or set-up costs such as the cost of the winder and its material handling
accessories and the autofrettage/hydrotest facility and installation.

reserves, cost of working capital, and inbound shipping costs of materials. This cost is

2.6.5 Life Assessment of the Vessel

      Fatigue analysis of the wire jacket was performed using methods described in KD-

  2.7  Objective 6: Commercialization and TRL Assessment

 WireTough has conceived, designed, produced, and obtained certification for a durable,
all-steel Type II-S cylinder constructed using widely available materials for storing bulk
hydrogen at high pressures. The cylinder was certified under ASME Section VIII-Division
3  rules  for  cylinders  for ground storage  of hydrogen. The  cylinder  is ready  for
commercialization in the hydrogen storage market.

 The cylinder operates at a maximum operating pressure (MOP) of 350 bar, has a cyclic
life of 6000 cycles (30 years at 200 cycles/year) and can store 40.7 KG hydrogen in a
single unit (four times that of a Type 1). Further, it is 50% lighter in weight than Type 1
cylinders, has a higher fatigue life and has comparable durability against impact and
ballistic loading.

 The manufacturing costs of this Type II-S cylinder is estimated at $827/KG of hydrogen
stored compared to the price of $1604 per KG of hydrogen stored for Type I cylinders.
WireTough has reached the conclusion that the best way to commercialize this product
is through partnerships with large scale manufacturers, specially Type 1 cylinder

   A. TECHNOLOGY READINESS LEVEL

   The objective of the project was to design and produce a full-scale prototype of a pressure
vessel for storing 40 to 50 kilograms of gaseous hydrogen at an operating pressure of 350 to 500
bar (6,525 to 7,250 psi). The goal was to design the pressure vessel as per applicable ASME design
codes, produce a full-scale prototype of the vessel to demonstrate the manufacturing processes,
and test the vessel to demonstrate full compliance with ASME Section VIII- Division 3 rules for
certification. WireTough’s assessment of the technology readiness level at the start of the program
was TRL-4 as explained below.
   At the beginning of the program, the construction materials for use in Type II-S cylinders to
store hydrogen at high pressures were known through research/technology development work
performed by Sandia National Laboratory over two decades that are incorporated in ASME Design
Codes, and work performed by WireTough as part of a previous DOE sponsored program6.
WireTough also had previous experience in the design, manufacturing, and commercialization of
smaller Type II-S pressure vessels for storing CNG and air using the same construction materials
and proprietary manufacturing processes. This justified the TRL-4 level designation at the start of
the project. This level was raised to TRL-8 with the work performed as part of this project.

    B. WORK COMPLETED DURING THE PROJECT

   WireTough’s approach for designing pressure vessels for hydrogen storage at high pressures
consists of using conventional steel liners and wrapping them with commercially available ultra-
high strength, 0.35mm (0.014”) diameter steel wires using its proprietary wrapping processes. The
steel liner selected for this project was 610 mm (24”) OD and 7600 mm  (25.5’) long, with a water
capacity of 1715 L. The minimum wall thickness of the liner was 22.9 mm, and  it weighs
approximately 2700 KG. During this project, the following work was completed to justify the new
TRL rating:

    Liners made from SA 372 Grade E Class 70 steel approved for use by ASME design codes in
high pressure hydrogen storage applications were procured for the project as per specifications
prepared by WireTough. The liners were made from seamless tubes with swaged ends and
contained no welds, an ASME code requirement. See Fig. 1 for a picture of the liners procured.
   WireTough used its prior experience in designing and prototyping pressure vessels to develop
a preliminary design for the cylinder as part of this project. The design was evaluated using state-
of-the-art finite element analyses, advanced design optimization software tools, and fracture
mechanics analyses by the Siemens Corporate Technology Center, Charlotte, NC.

   The stresses generated on the liner during wire winding and pressurization of the cylinder
during autofrettage were estimated by finite element analyses. The autofrettage pressure chosen
was sufficient to deform the liner plastically while the wire jacket remained elastic. Upon releasing
the autofrettage pressure, the wire jacket exerted high residual compressive hoop stresses on the
liner walls, Fig. 2. Subsequently, the cylinder was pressurized to the maximum operating pressure
(MOP) to estimate the cyclic stresses during service. These cyclic stresses were used to estimate
the design life of the vessel using fracture mechanics. The design was tested against all possible
failure  criteria required under ASME Section VIII- Division 3 design codes. From these
evaluations, it was shown that cylinder can hold 40.7 KG of H2 at a maximum operating pressure
of 350 bar and can be safely cycled for 6000 cycles between MOP and the 10% of MOP prior to
re-inspection. At a rate of 200 cycles per year, this will take 30 years. The critical region of the
cylinder that limits its cyclic life is the exposed area of the liner in the dome region and is accessible
for ultrasonic inspection for extending its service life beyond 6000 cycles.
  A user design specification (UDS) and a manufacturer’s design report (MDR) were prepared
by Structural Integrity Associates (SIA) who independently verified the integrity of the design and
certified it using design criteria and rules established under ASME Section VIII-Division 3 design
code. This cleared the way for manufacturing two full-scale prototype vessels.

cylinder was produced in WireTough’s Bristol facility, see Fig. 3 for a picture of wrapped cylinder.
